# Data Visualization and Infographic Storytelling Layout

## Overview

A structured layout for communicating quantitative information, research findings, comparisons, processes, and evidence through clear visual storytelling. It combines data hierarchy, chart selection, explanatory typography, and editorial composition.

The template prioritizes accurate interpretation and readability over decoration. It can be used independently or combined with any compatible TypeCraft typography style.

## Intended Purpose

Use this template for:
- Statistical and research infographics.
- Business and organizational performance summaries.
- Educational data explainers.
- Survey and market research visualizations.
- Comparative data graphics.
- Public information and awareness campaigns.
- Digital dashboards and editorial data stories.

Choose another template when the primary objective is a conventional presentation deck, annual report, or promotional poster rather than a focused data narrative.

## Layout Architecture

Organize the composition into five functional zones:

1. **Headline zone:** State the central subject or finding.
2. **Context zone:** Explain the question, scope, source, and relevant timeframe.
3. **Evidence zone:** Present the primary chart, comparison, map, diagram, or quantitative display.
4. **Interpretation zone:** Explain the important pattern, contrast, or implication.
5. **Source zone:** Provide data attribution, methodology notes, definitions, and qualifications.

The evidence zone should receive the strongest visual emphasis. Supporting elements must help the reader understand it rather than compete with it.

Use a modular grid appropriate to the output format. Establish consistent alignment for chart edges, headings, annotations, legends, and supporting text.

For multiple findings, organize content into a clear reading sequence instead of compressing unrelated charts into one crowded composition.

## Content Hierarchy

Use this priority:

1. Main finding or central question.
2. Primary data visualization.
3. Key values, comparisons, or changes.
4. Explanation and contextual annotations.
5. Supporting evidence and secondary visualizations.
6. Data source, methodology, definitions, and caveats.

Write headlines that accurately describe the evidence. Do not exaggerate a correlation, imply causation without evidence, or present an estimate as an exact measurement.

Keep labels close to the data they explain. Avoid forcing readers to repeatedly move between distant legends and chart elements.

## Typography Placement

Use typography to distinguish:
- Main headline.
- Section headings.
- Key figures and units.
- Axis labels and category names.
- Chart annotations.
- Explanatory paragraphs.
- Sources, footnotes, and methodology.

Use highly legible type for numerical labels and small supporting text. Keep number formatting, decimal precision, units, and date formats consistent throughout the composition.

Use tabular numerals when available for aligned numerical comparisons. Avoid decorative typefaces for dense labels, axes, and data tables.

Typography must not distort the perceived importance of values or make secondary data appear more significant than the evidence supports.

## Color and Background

Use color systematically to encode meaningful categories, series, or status.

- Maintain consistent category colors across related charts.
- Use sufficient contrast between data marks and the background.
- Do not rely on color alone to communicate distinctions.
- Use direct labels, patterns, symbols, or line styles where needed.
- Reserve strong accent colors for important findings or meaningful comparisons.
- Use neutral backgrounds when they improve chart readability.

For sequential data, use an ordered visual progression. For diverging data, make the midpoint and direction clear. For categorical data, choose distinguishable colors without implying an unsupported ranking.

Check contrast and color accessibility, including common color-vision deficiencies.

## Spacing and Alignment

Maintain consistent spacing between:
- Headline and context.
- Charts and annotations.
- Data labels and visual marks.
- Separate findings or sections.
- Main content and source notes.

Align chart plotting areas and comparison baselines when direct comparison is intended.

Avoid unnecessary decorative shapes, excessive gridlines, redundant legends, and empty visual containers. Use whitespace to separate ideas and clarify the reading order.

Keep footnotes and source information readable rather than reducing them to illegible microtext.

## Responsive Adaptation

For wide layouts:
- Use multiple columns when comparisons remain easy to follow.
- Place related charts side by side with aligned scales where appropriate.
- Keep annotations near their corresponding evidence.

For portrait and mobile layouts:
- Recompose into a single-column reading sequence.
- Stack charts and supporting explanations.
- Simplify labels carefully without removing essential context.
- Avoid shrinking complex charts until their values become unreadable.

For motion graphics:
- Reveal the question, evidence, and interpretation in a logical sequence.
- Preserve enough screen time for values and labels to be read.
- Keep axes, units, and category identities stable during transitions.
- Do not animate data in a way that implies false magnitude or causation.

For web interfaces:
- Provide accessible text alternatives or summaries.
- Preserve essential data and context at smaller widths.
- Support readable labels, tooltips, and interaction without hiding critical information.

## Customization Boundaries

Users may customize:
- Canvas dimensions and orientation.
- Number and arrangement of charts.
- Chart types appropriate to the data.
- Typography and visual style.
- Color palette and category encoding.
- Annotation density.
- Reading sequence and narrative emphasis.

Do not:
- Invent, alter, or fabricate data.
- Remove units, baselines, or qualifications needed for interpretation.
- Use misleading axis ranges or inconsistent scales.
- Imply causation from correlation alone.
- Hide uncertainty or material limitations.
- Use area, volume, or perspective to exaggerate quantitative differences.
- Remove source attribution when the data requires it.

If data is missing or ambiguous, identify the limitation instead of filling it with invented values.

## Domain Applications

**Static graphics:** Research infographics, statistical posters, educational explainers, and public information graphics.

**Editorial:** Data journalism, magazine charts, research summaries, and explanatory visual essays.

**Web and UI:** Dashboard sections, analytics summaries, interactive charts, and data-focused landing pages.

**Motion graphics:** Animated statistics, evidence-led explainers, and sequential data stories.

Adapt chart complexity, annotations, and reading order to the audience and delivery medium.

## Limitations

This template provides a composition and communication framework. It does not verify the accuracy, completeness, or provenance of a dataset.

Complex statistical analysis, specialized scientific visualization, geographic projections, and interactive dashboard engineering may require domain-specific tools and expert review.

A visually attractive chart is not necessarily an accurate chart. Validate the underlying data, units, scales, calculations, and interpretation before publication.

## Execution Instructions

1. Identify the audience, communication objective, and central question.
2. Inspect the supplied dataset and identify its variables, units, timeframe, and limitations.
3. Determine the main evidence and the most appropriate chart or visual representation.
4. Establish a clear headline and reading sequence.
5. Build the evidence zone using accurate scales, labels, and units.
6. Add annotations that explain meaningful patterns without overstating conclusions.
7. Apply the selected typography style, color system, and grid.
8. Adapt the composition to the target medium and screen size.
9. Verify every displayed value against the source data.
10. Check accessibility, legibility, source attribution, and methodological qualifications.

If the user explicitly selects a chart type, style, or layout constraint, preserve that choice unless it makes the visualization materially misleading. Explain any necessary correction before changing it.

## Quality Criteria

A successful implementation must:
- Communicate one clear central question or finding.
- Represent the supplied data accurately.
- Use an appropriate chart or visual encoding.
- Maintain a clear hierarchy between headline, evidence, and interpretation.
- Label values, units, categories, and timeframes correctly.
- Avoid misleading scales and unsupported conclusions.
- Remain legible at the intended display size.
- Use color consistently and accessibly.
- Include appropriate source and methodology information.
- Adapt cleanly to the target medium.
- Preserve the user's explicit creative and data requirements.
